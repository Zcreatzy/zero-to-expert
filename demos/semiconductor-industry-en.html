How Chips Create Global Industry Value

This atlas starts with transistors and workloads, moves through design, manufacturing, yield, packaging, profit pools, and industry cycles, and ends with standards, resources, and geopolitical constraints. The goal is not to memorize company names, but to judge where bottlenecks sit, why value migrates, and what stage a reported “breakthrough” has actually reached.

01 · 60-second orientation

Start with one master model: the chip industry sells constrained system capability

Scope and boundaries
Included:Global chip design, EDA/IP, manufacturing, equipment and materials, packaging and test, product markets, business models, standards, policy, and the 2026 frontier.
Excluded:Company-by-company valuation, investment advice, full device mathematics, every process recipe, and military-chip details. Physics is the bridge that explains constraints; the industrial system is the narrative spine.

02 · 10-minute foundation

Eight concepts are enough to enter the industry conversation

Transistor

A device that uses voltage to control current. Digital circuits organize transistors into switching networks; analog circuits use continuous responses to process sensing, RF, and power signals.

Process node

Labels such as 3 nm and 2 nm denote process generations, not a single physical dimension. Node comparisons must return to performance, power, area, cost, yield, and maturity.

Wafer / die

Many rectangular dies are patterned repeatedly on a circular wafer. Larger dies mean fewer per wafer and usually a higher chance that a random defect hits each die.

Yield

The share of dies that meet specification. Yield translates nanoscale manufacturing capability into unit cost and delivery capacity; a successful tape-out is far from economical mass production.

PPA + C

Performance, Power, Area, plus Cost: optimize performance, power, area, and cost together; automotive and industrial applications must also include reliability and lifetime explicitly.

ISA ≠ microarchitecture

The instruction set is the software-visible interface. One ISA can have in-order, out-of-order, low-power, or high-performance implementations. The RISC-V specification explicitly separates the ISA from implementation.[4]

Foundry / Fabless / IDM / OSAT

These models emphasize contract manufacturing, fabless design, integrated design and manufacturing, and outsourced assembly and test. They are business archetypes, not mutually exclusive identities.

Chiplet

Combine dies with different functions and process technologies inside one package. Benefits include module reuse and system scaling; costs include more complex interconnect, thermal, test, yield, and accountability boundaries.

Abstraction stack: do not compare CPUs, Arm, 3 nm, and advanced packaging at the same layer

Profit pools ≠ capital expenditure

Design can capture high value through replicable IP; manufacturing can earn high profits through process capability and scale. Traditional assembly and test has lower value added, but AI advanced packaging may shift local bargaining power. OECD aggregates are structural clues, not direct profit-margin estimates.[23]

Moats usually span layers

Architectural performance, software toolchains, manufacturing availability, customer qualification, and supply assurance jointly raise switching costs. For analog and embedded chips, product lifetime and channels often matter more than the newest node.[24]

Companies are coordinates, not a curriculum

To understand the value of TSMC, ASML, NVIDIA, Arm, Micron, or TI, first ask which scarce interface or capability it controls, then whether that scarcity can persist across cycles.

What matters:Area determines how many dies fit on a wafer; defect density determines how many pass. Their interaction creates nonlinear cost pressure. One economic motivation for chiplets is to avoid placing every function on one giant die, though packaging and test costs rise.

Test ≠ reliability

Wafer sort and final test find present failures; qualification and reliability monitoring study long-term failure under temperature, time, mechanical, and electrical stress. Advanced packaging also needs layer alignment, thermal characterization, and 3D test access.[7]

Production is a sequence of states

Research demonstration, sampling, risk production, production entry, high-volume manufacturing, stable yield, and broad deployment are not synonyms. A public claim of “good yield” without a denominator cannot be compared across fabs.

Packaging becomes architecture

Chiplets move system scaling into the package. UCIe 2.0/3.0 adds interconnect, management, test, and 3D support to an open specification, but publication does not equal multi-vendor plug-and-play.[8]

Foundry: capital intensity can still yield high profits

TSMC reported a 59.9% gross margin and 50.8% operating margin in 2025; 7 nm and more advanced technologies represented 74% of wafer revenue. Process capability, scale, customer mix, and utilization can create a manufacturing profit pool. This is one company, not every foundry.[9]

Equipment: scarce tools + installed-base service

ASML recorded €32.7 billion in 2025 sales and a 52.8% gross margin; service and field-option revenue was €8.2 billion. Experts track not just new-tool shipments but also the installed base, upgrades, and service.[10]

Fixed costs amplify swings

Much of a fab’s cost does not fall with short-term demand. Low utilization rapidly erodes unit economics; rising yield and utilization turn the same capacity into more qualified output.[24]

Minimum company-comparison set:For fabless firms, track design wins, product mix, R&D efficiency, and ecosystem. For foundries/IDMs, track utilization, yield, node/product mix, depreciation, and capital intensity. For equipment vendors, track orders, installed base, service, and customer capex. For memory, track bit growth, pricing, inventory, and supply discipline.

06 · Cycle mechanics

Why can small demand changes produce huge profit swings?

Scroll horizontally to view the cycle feedback →

2025 was not a synchronized industry boom

In final WSTS data, computing applications grew more than 60% year over year, industrial grew about 5%, and discrete devices remained slightly negative for the year. Logic, memory, and AI-related computing drove the overall surge.[1]

Inventory exists at three layers

Distributor, system-customer, and chip-supplier inventories are different. Cycle analysis must combine end-market sell-through, channel inventory, cancellations, lead times, utilization, and pricing.

Capex is not demand

Capex represents capacity construction and equipment purchases. It may validate long-term confidence or deepen a downturn when supply arrives together. Watch production timing, yield, customer qualification, and free cash flow.

07 · Key inflection points

Every breakthrough removes one bottleneck and pushes the constraint to another layer

1947–1959
Transistor, planar process, and integrated circuit
Solid-state switches replaced vacuum tubes, and multiple devices could be manufactured together on one substrate. The new problem became scaling integration reliably.
1965–1970s
Scaling and the microprocessor platform
Long-term trends in transistor count and unit cost shaped industry expectations; general-purpose CPUs made software an amplifier of hardware value.
1980s–1990s
EDA, pure-play foundry, and fabless
Design complexity and fab capital intensity drove specialization. TSMC’s pure-play foundry model, established in 1987, became a pivotal institutional inflection.[9]
2000s
End of Dennard scaling, the power wall, and multicore
Frequency could no longer rise continuously at low cost; architectural parallelism, specialized acceleration, and efficiency became central.
2010s
FinFET, EUV, and mobile/cloud
Devices became three-dimensional, EUV entered advanced layers, and software ecosystems plus hyperscale data centers reshaped product profit pools.
2020s
GAA, backside power, HBM, chiplets, and 3D
Scaling has shifted from a single-die path to co-engineering devices, interconnects, packaging, memory, power delivery, and cooling. The IRDS roadmap identifies GAA and backside power as important routes for continued scaling.[19]

08 · Current state and frontier

As of 2026-08-08: advanced computing is pushing bottlenecks to system boundaries

N2 has entered high-volume manufacturing

TSMC disclosed that N2 entered high-volume manufacturing in Q4 2025. N2P and A16 with Super Power Rail backside power target volume production in the second half of 2026. The first is an observed state; the latter remain company targets.[9]

2 nm has begun generating revenue

TSMC’s Q2 2026 report shows 2 nm at 3% of wafer revenue and 7 nm and below at 77%, distinguishing “entered production” from “visible commercial contribution.”[11]

HBM4 moves from samples to volume production

On 2026-03-16, Micron said its 36 GB 12-high HBM4 began volume shipments in Q1, with vendor-reported bandwidth above 2.8 TB/s. This directly establishes that product’s state, not industry-wide supply-demand or cross-vendor leadership.[17]

UCIe 3.0 raises speed to 64 GT/s

The specification released on 2025-08-05 also added management, throttling, and power-saving capabilities. Standards reduce interface friction, but open specifications, interoperability certification, and large-scale multi-vendor shipments remain three distinct stages.[8]

High-NA: delivered does not mean broadly deployed

ASML’s 2026 AGM materials report eight High-NA systems delivered and six operational by year-end 2025, target HVM readiness by year-end 2026, and point to customer insertion in 2027–2028. The latter two are forward-looking statements.[18]

Value is migrating toward bottleneck combinations

When the compute die is no longer the sole constraint, HBM, packaging capacity, interconnect, power, cooling, networking, and software utilization jointly determine system delivery. Whether profit pools migrate depends on bottleneck duration and substitution speed. This is a synthesis based on HBM4 and chiplet/packaging progress.[17][7]

Frontier judgment:GAA, backside power, High-NA, hybrid bonding, optical interconnect, and 3D stacking are not mutually exclusive substitutes. Different layers address different constraints. The key questions are: in which workload does the benefit materialize, and where does the cost move?

09 · Standards, policy, resources, and geopolitics

Technical feasibility does not guarantee compliant, scalable, cross-border delivery

87%

Operating wafer capacity is concentrated

The OECD reports that as of September 2025, China, Chinese Taipei, Korea, Japan, and the United States together held 87% of global operating wafer-fabrication capacity. Wafers per month excludes yield, die area, and customer qualification, so it is not directly interchangeable output.[14]

24 + 3

Controls expand across layers

The U.S. BIS rule of 2024-12-02 added controls on 24 types of manufacturing equipment, three software-tool categories, and HBM, plus 140 Entity List additions. The source establishes rule content and policy intent, not final effects independently.[12]

40

Projects must be judged by milestones

GAO reported that as of July 2025, U.S. CHIPS awards covered 19 companies, 40 projects, and $30.9 billion in direct funding; milestones extend as far as 2033. Announced dollars are not operating capacity.[13]

Water, power, and infrastructure

A NIST environmental assessment gives both a 2021 median ultrapure-water use of 4.25 billion gallons per year and 1.16 million gallons per day for 29 U.S. facilities. These differ arithmetically by about 10× (the former is about 11.64 million/day). This atlas treats them only as an order-of-magnitude signal—millions to tens of millions of gallons/day—not as a reliable exact conversion or a value for every fab type.[15]

Fluorinated gases and hazardous chemicals

Etch and deposition-chamber cleaning use high-global-warming-potential fluorinated gases. EPA also lists HCl, HF, glycol ethers, methanol, and xylene among major hazardous air pollutants.[16][26]

New fabs do not automatically remove material concentration

USGS estimates that China produced 99% of global primary low-purity gallium in 2025. Gallium is used in GaAs/GaN devices. This measure does not equal all high-purity refined material or usable wafer supply.[20]

Four scenarios, not one-path forecasting

A · AI / memory supercycle

Condition:End deployment and returns persist while HBM and advanced-packaging expansion still trails demand.
Leading indicators:Actual sales, memory prices, packaging lead times, system utilization, and customer free cash flow.[1][17]

B · Digestion after expansion

Condition:Multiple projects enter production together while real demand or customer qualification lags.
Leading indicators:Planned → construction → production conversion, yield, utilization, inventory, and cancellations.[13][14]

C · Regionalized technology stacks

Condition:Controls expand, allied rules converge, and circumvention plus domestic substitution proceed together.
Leading indicators:Licenses, third-country trade, tool installation, standards/toolchain divergence, and compliance cost.[12][2]

D · Infrastructure constrains expansion

Condition:Water, power, wastewater, talent, and permits lag the fab shell.
Leading indicators:Utility agreements, equipment hookup, hiring, environmental permits, and resource intensity per good die.[15]

Definition conflict: 791.7 vs 795.6

SIA’s 2026-02-06 release reported 2025 sales of $791.7 billion using data available then; WSTS updated the figure to $795.6 billion on 2026-03-06 with final Q4 data. Neither is “lying”; they use different data vintages. Use the final value while preserving the revision trail.[21][1]

Five-layer evidence filter

  1. Science:Is the mechanism possible, and what are the boundary conditions?
  2. Engineering:Is it repeatable, and is the benchmark fair?
  3. Manufacturing:Yield, reliability, scale, and test?
  4. Commercial:Customers, revenue, total cost of ownership, and cash flow?
  5. External:Supply, standards, resources, policy, and compliance?

Four leading indicators worth tracking

  1. Bottleneck migration:Lead times, utilization, yield, and HBM/packaging/power constraints.
  2. Status transitions:Samples → risk production → HVM → customer revenue.
  3. Capital efficiency:Whether capex becomes qualified capacity with customers.
  4. Substitutability:Whether a second source matches product, process, packaging, and qualification.

11 · From map to capability

2 hours / 2 days / 7 days: use AI to turn knowledge into falsifiable judgment

2 hours · Reconstruct the map

Close the page and draw the abstraction stack and value chain. Distinguish devices, ISA, microarchitecture, SoC, node, die, and package in your own words. Let AI question each item to expose gaps, then return to this page and primary sources to correct them.

2 days · Complete a comparison

Choose one compute chip and one analog/power chip. Use AI to build a comparison framework, but personally verify PPA, process, packaging, customer qualification, and business metrics. Complete a yield-sensitivity analysis and add a counterexample that overturns your initial view.

7 days · Make an independent judgment

Choose one “breakthrough” story, build a claim ledger, and write a one-page technical–commercial–policy analysis. Ask AI to red-team it as an engineer, customer, and skeptic, then revise using only traceable primary evidence.

Self-test: can you connect each answer into a causal chain without looking anything up?

  1. Why are ISA, microarchitecture, and process node not interchangeable?
  2. Why can cost rise nonlinearly as die area increases?
  3. Why can strong AI-accelerator demand shift bottlenecks to HBM, packaging, power, or software?
  4. Why does a growing total market not imply synchronized strength in analog, discrete, and industrial chips?
  5. How do you judge whether an announced fab actually improves supply-chain resilience?
  6. What evidence is missing between “High-NA delivered” and “High-NA broadly deployed in volume production”?
Show the strong-answer rubric

A strong answer connects at least three layers: physics/architecture constraints → manufacturing/yield/packaging → business metrics/customer value. It also identifies evidence state, denominator, source incentives, and falsifiable indicators. Repeating terms means you can converse; comparing tradeoffs means you can reason; identifying definitions and the next evidence means you can evaluate.

You should now be able to explain:How chips move from workloads to layouts and wafers; why yield connects physics and economics; why business models require different metrics; how inventory and fixed costs amplify cycles; and why policy, standards, and resources rewrite technology roadmaps.
Still requires practice:Device/circuit mathematics, EDA tools, process experiments, yield engineering, package thermal design, customer product definition, financial modeling, and real supplier negotiation.
