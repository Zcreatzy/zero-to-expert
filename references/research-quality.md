## Evidence architecture

Use sources according to the claim, not a single universal ranking.

| Claim type | Preferred evidence |
|---|---|
| Definition, standard, regulation | standards body, statute/regulator, official specification |
| Scientific mechanism or result | original paper plus recent review/meta-analysis when available |
| Technical capability | specification, benchmark methodology, independent reproduction or teardown |
| Company action or product | official filing/release/docs, then independent reporting for context |
| Market size or economics | regulator/statistical agency, filings, transparent methodology; disclose estimates |
| History | original document or reputable scholarly history |
| Consensus or controversy | multiple recent reviews, position statements, or independent expert sources |

Prefer primary sources for facts and high-quality syntheses for orientation. Use secondary reporting to connect facts, never as automatic proof of technical claims. Do not use search snippets as evidence.

## Search sequence

1. Search the topic plus “overview,” “review,” “handbook,” “standard,” or “industry structure” to learn vocabulary.
2. Replace the broad query with each question-tree branch.
3. Locate primary sources for the claims likely to appear in the opening, master visual, and expert conclusions.
4. Search explicitly for counterevidence: “limitations,” “failure,” “critique,” “replication,” “benchmark methodology,” or competing explanations.
5. Search recent developments by a bounded date range. Verify the event date separately from the publication date.
6. Stop when every high-importance claim has adequate support and new sources mostly repeat known information.

## Claim discipline

Keep a temporary ledger with these fields:

| ID | Claim | Why it matters | Source | Published | Event/data date | Type | Perspective/incentive | Confidence | Section |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Apply these labels consistently:

- **Foundation**: durable, well-supported background.
- **Current state**: observed and dated recent condition.
- **Frontier**: active work whose outcome is unresolved.
- **Debate**: credible disagreement about evidence, interpretation, or priorities.
- **Inference**: synthesis derived from cited facts; explain the reasoning.
- **Scenario**: conditional future, with assumptions and leading indicators.

Use calibrated language. “Demonstrated in a lab,” “announced,” “sampled,” “in production,” and “widely deployed” are different states. Preserve those distinctions.

## Recency and dates

- Put an “evidence checked through YYYY-MM-DD” stamp near the title.
- Give exact dates for recent milestones and policies. Use the data period for statistics.
- For a fast-moving topic, emphasize sources from the last 12–24 months while retaining older canonical sources.
- Treat roadmaps, targets, preprints, press releases, and analyst estimates as their stated evidence types.
- Do not call something “latest” unless the search covered the relevant current period.

## Citation design

- Add a superscript or bracketed citation link beside the supported claim.
- Link to the direct source page, DOI, standard, filing, or report—not a search result.
- Reuse a citation number for the same source.
- In the source library, record title, author/publisher, date, evidence type, and URL; optionally add one sentence explaining relevance.
- Identify source perspective when it affects interpretation: for example vendor specification, company filing, trade-association estimate, government policy position, consultancy forecast, or independent replication.
- Ensure every number, quotation, surprising causal claim, current-state claim, and named superlative is supported.
- Ensure each current-state, frontier, debate, forecast, and quantitative card has a nearby citation or is explicitly labeled as a synthesis/inference with its inputs cited.
- Clearly identify calculations or inferences made from multiple sources.

## Compression and stopping rules

Prioritize material that changes the reader's mental model or decisions. Remove:

- long lists without a comparison dimension;
- company or scholar name-dropping;
- repeated definitions;
- chronology without causal significance;
- numbers without a denominator, benchmark, or implication;
- decorative visuals that do not encode information.

For each module, aim for one governing question, one key takeaway, one explanatory visual, one concrete example, and optional deeper detail. If a section cannot justify its space, merge or delete it.
