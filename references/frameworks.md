## Shared learning ladder

Organize the reader's progression around capability rather than chapter count.

| Level | Reader can… | Required content |
|---|---|---|
| Orient | explain the field in plain language | definition, scope, stakes, master map |
| Converse | use the essential vocabulary correctly | 5–9 concepts, distinctions, examples |
| Reason | predict consequences inside the system | mechanism/stack, causal links, tradeoffs |
| Evaluate | compare claims, methods, or strategies | metrics, evidence quality, failure modes |
| Navigate | locate unknowns and choose next actions | frontier, source map, practice path |

Do not equate the final level with professional mastery. State which tacit, mathematical, laboratory, engineering, commercial, or regulatory skills require further practice.

## Academic framework

Use the following modules as the main narrative. Merge modules when the field is compact.

1. **Field in one view** — object of study, boundary, neighboring fields, central questions, and why the questions matter.
2. **Prerequisite bridge** — only the mathematics, science, or terminology needed for what follows; include a dependency map.
3. **Core objects and definitions** — 5–9 high-leverage concepts with non-examples and commonly confused pairs.
4. **First principles and mechanisms** — assumptions, causal structure, canonical equations/models, and an annotated worked example. Explain symbols and limits of each model.
5. **Paradigms and schools** — competing theories or research programs, what evidence each explains, and where each fails.
6. **Methods and evidence** — experimental/computational/theoretical methods, typical data, controls, benchmarks, uncertainty, replication, and what counts as convincing evidence.
7. **Intellectual history** — turning points rather than a list of dates; show what problem each advance resolved and what new question it opened.
8. **Canonical works and results** — a small, defensible set of textbooks, papers, datasets, theorems, experiments, or tools; explain why each is canonical.
9. **Current map of the literature** — active subfields, influential venues/groups when relevant, recent syntheses, and connections among clusters.
10. **Frontier and open questions** — separate widely accepted results, live disputes, bottlenecks, promising directions, and speculative bets. Date the assessment.
11. **Expert judgment** — how specialists detect weak assumptions, bad identification, metric gaming, overclaiming, or non-generalizable results.
12. **AI-assisted capability sprint** — 2 hours to reconstruct the field map, 2 days to complete worked cases and counterexamples, and 7 days to produce and defend an independent analysis. Pair AI critique with primary-source verification.

Recommended visuals: concept dependency graph, mechanism diagram, paradigm comparison table, methods/evidence pipeline, annotated equation or experiment, intellectual timeline, frontier uncertainty map.

## Technology–industry framework

Use the following modules as the main narrative. Distinguish technical feasibility from commercial viability.

1. **Industry in one view** — customer need, delivered value, boundary, market context, and the master flow from inputs to outcomes.
2. **Technical foundations** — the minimum science/engineering needed to understand products and constraints.
3. **Architecture and stack** — layers, interfaces, dependencies, design choices, bottlenecks, and build-versus-buy boundaries.
4. **How it is made and operated** — R&D, supply chain, production, deployment, quality/yield/reliability, distribution, maintenance, and end-of-life where applicable.
5. **Products and use cases** — segment by job-to-be-done or customer, not by arbitrary vendor categories. Include representative unit economics or performance equations when useful.
6. **Value chain and profit pools** — suppliers, complements, channels, customers, switching costs, bargaining power, capital intensity, and where value accrues.
7. **Competitive landscape** — archetypes and representative organizations; compare capabilities, positioning, dependencies, and durable advantages rather than listing company facts.
8. **Metrics and economics** — technical KPIs, cost curves, utilization, yield, margins, cycles, adoption drivers, and the metrics insiders watch. Define denominators and dates.
9. **History and inflection points** — standards, process or architectural shifts, cost thresholds, policy shocks, business-model changes, and demand transitions.
10. **Standards, policy, and geopolitics** — applicable regulation, safety, IP, trade controls, environmental constraints, and geographic concentration. Avoid a generic section if immaterial.
11. **Current state and recent progress** — dated product/technical milestones, market changes, deals, policy moves, and adoption signals. Separate announcements from deployed reality.
12. **Frontier scenarios** — bottlenecks, roadmaps, plausible scenarios, leading indicators, and explicit assumptions. Do not present forecasts as facts.
13. **Expert judgment** — how practitioners test vendor claims, spot benchmark tricks, distinguish demos from scalable systems, and reason about second-order effects.
14. **AI-assisted capability sprint** — 2 hours to reconstruct the stack and vocabulary, 2 days to complete teardowns or comparisons, and 7 days to deliver and defend an independent analysis or build. Pair AI critique with primary-source verification.

Recommended visuals: layered stack, value-chain/Sankey-like flow, manufacturing or service lifecycle, performance–cost tradeoff, competitive matrix, KPI dashboard, cycle timeline, scenario tree.

## Mixed-route composition

Choose one framework as the spine. Insert no more than four modules from the secondary framework. Typical patterns:

- **Research becoming an industry**: academic spine + readiness levels, stack, value chain, adoption bottlenecks.
- **Deep-tech industry**: industry spine + first principles, research methods, frontier literature, open scientific questions.
- **Regulated applied science**: industry spine + evidence standards, trial/validation methods, research uncertainty.
- **Computing platform/ecosystem**: industry spine + formal models or algorithms where they explain architectural tradeoffs.

Label transitions between scientific confidence, engineering feasibility, operational scalability, and commercial adoption.

## Topic classification examples

| Request | Route | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| “Teach me condensed-matter physics” | Academic | theories, methods, and literature dominate |
| “Explain the chip industry” | Technology–industry | stack, manufacturing, firms, economics, and geopolitics dominate |
| “Explain semiconductor devices” | Mixed, academic spine | physics is central; fabrication and applications add context |
| “Map the quantum-computing industry” | Mixed, industry spine | ecosystem and roadmaps dominate but scientific uncertainty is essential |
| “Get me up to speed on CRISPR research” | Academic | mechanisms, methods, evidence, and frontier dominate |
| “Teach me the EV battery business” | Technology–industry | product stack, supply chain, unit economics, and policy dominate |
